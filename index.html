PHIL 225 Exam 2 Course Guide

Purpose. This course guide supports the PHIL 225 Exam 2 correction process. It identifies the authorized exam questions, course-specific interpretations of the assigned authors, student PowerPoint locations, Blackboard reading references, common misconceptions, and the standards for demonstrating mastery.

Course scope. The five topics are Don Marquis on the wrongness of killing, Judith Jarvis Thomson on bodily rights, Hugh LaFollette on licensing parents, Frances Kamm's response to Michael Sandel on enhancement, and John Corvino on arguments from naturalness.

Assigned materials. Students should consult the student-facing PowerPoints and the exact assigned versions of the readings posted in Blackboard. Reading titles and page ranges on this page are locators, not substitutes for the assigned texts. Because some student slides are note-taking aids, the reading references supply the fuller context needed for reconstruction in the student's own words.

Authorized Exam Questions

  1. Marquis: What is Marquis's theory of the wrongness of killing, and how does he bring this theory to bear on abortion? List three merits. List one objection and explain Marquis's response.
  2. Thomson: Explain two of Thomson's thought experiments and what each is supposed to imply for abortion.
  3. LaFollette: What are the three standard criteria for licensing, how does LaFollette apply them to parents, and how does he address the parental-rights objection?
  4. Kamm: How does Kamm respond to Sandel's claims that enhancement will create harmful forms of responsibility and decrease our obligation to others?
  5. Corvino: Choose three meanings of “unnatural” and explain why they do not show that homosexuality is wrong.

Bonus, 5 points, not correctable: Why is Thomson's argument not an argument for a right to secure the death of the unborn child?

Q1: Don Marquis

Required answer components

  • Killing is prima facie seriously wrong primarily because it deprives the victim of a valuable future.
  • The future includes experiences, activities, projects, relationships, enjoyments, and values the victim would later come to have.
  • A standard fetus has a future of the same morally relevant kind; abortion therefore causes the same kind of loss.
  • The conclusion is prima facie or presumptive, not an exceptionless claim.
  • A complete response explains any three merits: not restricted to biological humans; possible animal implications; compatibility with some euthanasia; straightforward account of killing infants/children.
  • A complete response explains one objection and Marquis’s response. Contraception: no nonarbitrarily identifiable existing subject is deprived. Interests: lack of a present subjective desire does not eliminate an objective interest in a future.
  • The argument should be distinguished from personhood and potential-personhood accounts.

Common misconceptions

  • “The fetus may become a person.”
  • “Every prevented possible future is a harmed subject.”
  • “The theory says every abortion is absolutely wrong.”
  • Merely naming merits without explaining why they support the theory.

Mastery criteria

  1. Explain the deprivation account and abortion application, including the prima-facie qualification.
  2. Explain three distinct merits.
  3. Explain one objection and Marquis's response.
  4. Correct a potential-personhood or contraception misconception without notes.

Q2: Judith Jarvis Thomson

Required answer components

  • Thomson grants fetal personhood for argument and challenges the inference from right to life to a right to another person's bodily support.
  • A complete response accurately describes two cases and connects each case to pregnancy.
  • Each case requires its distinctive implication, not merely the generic conclusion that abortion is allowed.
  • Acceptable cases include violinist, tiny house, Smith's coat, Henry Fonda, chocolates, burglar, people seeds, and Samaritan cases.
  • Thomson gives neither a universal yes nor a universal no.

Case implications

  • Violinist: need does not automatically create a right to bodily use.
  • Tiny house: an innocent threat may be resisted in self-defense.
  • Smith's coat: bodily ownership defeats false claims of third-party neutrality.
  • Henry Fonda: needing assistance for life does not itself establish a right to receive it.
  • Chocolates: indecency and injustice differ.
  • Burglar/people seeds: voluntary action and known risk do not automatically confer a right, especially when precautions fail.
  • Samaritans: minimal decency may be required without requiring enormous sacrifice.

Mastery criteria

  1. Explain the argumentative strategy.
  2. Describe and interpret two cases separately.
  3. Explain why the conclusion is qualified.

Q3: Hugh LaFollette

Required answer components

  • Serious potential harm: incompetent parenting can severely harm vulnerable children.
  • Competence: safe parenting requires minimal knowledge, judgment, temperament, stability, and ability.
  • Assessability: there must be a moderately reliable and fair way to determine competence; perfection is not required.
  • A complete response applies all three criteria to parenting and distinguishes competence from assessability.
  • Rights objection: parenting is claimed as a fundamental right.
  • Response: important rights may be limited to prevent harm; “right to have children” is ambiguous; reproduction, custody, and rearing are distinct; a plausible rearing right is conditional on minimal competence and nonabuse.

Common misconceptions

  • Calling assessability another parenting skill.
  • Requiring a perfect test.
  • Saying LaFollette denies all parental rights.
  • Treating licensing as a simple ban on biological reproduction.

Mastery criteria

  1. Explain and apply the three criteria.
  2. Distinguish competence from assessability through a fresh example.
  3. Explain the parental-rights objection and LaFollette's response.

Q4: Frances Kamm responding to Michael Sandel

Question 4 exact exam wording

How does Kamm respond to Sandel's claims that enhancement will create harmful forms of responsibility and decrease our obligation to others?

Question 4 student deck and slide mapping

Student deck: Genetic Engineering and Screening.
Slides 22-23: Sandel's responsibility and decreased-obligation/solidarity concerns.
Slide 24: Kamm's responsibility-does-not-eliminate-aid reply, including the hairdryer and conscience examples.
Slide 25: Kamm's fewer-needs/more-resources reply, the course's “embarrassment of riches” point.

Assigned Blackboard readings: Michael Sandel, The Case Against Perfection, especially pp. 60-62; Frances Kamm, Is There a Problem With Enhancement?, especially pp. 12-13.

Question 4 authoritative course account

  • Sandel's responsibility concern: As enhancement expands control, people may be blamed for traits they did not choose to acquire or remove.
  • Sandel's decreased-obligation and solidarity concern: If disadvantages appear chosen or avoidable, society may feel less obligated to share costs or assist. Sandel connects awareness of luck and giftedness with solidarity.
  • Kamm's first reply: Blameworthiness and responsibility for bearing costs are conceptually distinct. A person can be at fault without forfeiting an otherwise valid claim to lifesaving aid.
  • Hairdryer and conscience examples: Minor carelessness causing grave injury does not automatically erase a claim to medical care. Likewise, refusing an available enhancement or choosing from conscience does not necessarily forfeit assistance.
  • Kamm's second reply: Even if choice sometimes weakens claims to aid, widespread enhancement could produce fewer badly-off people and fewer claims on shared resources, leaving more for those who still need assistance. This is the course's “embarrassment of riches” point.
  • Limited conclusion: Kamm does not prove attitudes cannot worsen or guarantee solidarity. Kamm defeats the claim that reduced obligation necessarily follows from enhancement.

Question 4 required answer components

  1. Explain Sandel's harmful-responsibility concern.
  2. Explain how Sandel connects responsibility to decreased solidarity or social obligation.
  3. Explain Kamm's distinction between blameworthiness and who should bear costs.
  4. Explain Kamm's first reply using the hairdryer or conscience example.
  5. Explain the fewer-needs/more-resources or “embarrassment of riches” reply and its limited force.

Question 4 common misconceptions

  • Describes enhancement generally without explaining Sandel's two claims.
  • Says Kamm thinks responsibility is irrelevant.
  • Says responsibility automatically eliminates or never affects claims to aid.
  • Says Kamm proves everyone will remain generous or that enhancement has no moral risks.
  • Says “embarrassment of riches” means enhanced people become wealthy.

Question 4 mastery criteria

  1. Explain Sandel's responsibility concern.
  2. Explain Sandel's link from responsibility to decreased obligation or solidarity.
  3. Explain Kamm's distinction between blame and who bears costs, using the hairdryer or conscience example.
  4. Explain the fewer-needs/more-resources or “embarrassment of riches” reply.
  5. Correct the claim that Kamm believes enhancement has no moral risks or guarantees solidarity.

Q5: John Corvino

The phrase “unnatural” is ambiguous. A complete response chooses three meanings and gives the specific critique associated with each.

  1. Unusual/abnormal: statistical rarity does not imply immorality; many rare activities are harmless.
  2. Not practiced by other animals: the factual premise is false, and animal behavior is not a complete human moral standard.
  3. Not proceeding from innate desires: innate origin does not make a desire good, and noninnate origin does not make conduct wrong. Causal origin does not settle moral permissibility.
  4. Violates an organ's principal purpose: organs have multiple functions; intimacy and pleasure can be legitimate; consistency would condemn many accepted nonprocreative sexual acts.
  5. Disgusting/offensive: disgust is an aesthetic reaction, not a reliable moral test; many morally neutral activities provoke disgust.

Mastery criteria

  1. Identify three genuinely distinct meanings without being given the list.
  2. Explain the matching critique of each.
  3. Apply one critique to a fresh claim and explain why “unnatural” alone is insufficient.

Correction Credit and Validation

  • The instructor-assigned original score remains fixed and is used only for recovery arithmetic.
  • A mastered question recovers one-half of the originally lost points: (20 - original score) / 2. A skipped or incomplete question earns no recovery.
  • After Questions 1-5, the original question scores are added. The Thomson bonus ranges from 0 to 5, remains unchanged, and is not correctable.
  • The question subtotal plus the Thomson bonus must equal the official Exam 2 total. Any unexplained discrepancy requires instructor review.
  • Total recovery of 15 or more requires oral verification.

Bonus

Thomson argues for a right to end unwanted bodily support, not a separate right to ensure death. If separation does not kill the fetus or violinist, the person may not then kill merely to secure death.
